Breathing to calm down when you feel anxious

Anxiety speeds your breathing up before you notice it has happened. Slowing it back down on purpose, with a breath out that is longer than the breath in, is one of the few useful things you can do in thirty seconds, anywhere, without anyone else knowing you are doing it.

When you are anxious, breathing usually becomes faster, shallower and higher in the chest. That is a normal part of the body preparing for something, not a fault in you. The awkward part is that fast, shallow breathing produces sensations of its own — a tight chest, tingling fingers, a light head, a heart you can suddenly feel — and those sensations read as more evidence that something is wrong. The worry feeds the breathing and the breathing feeds the worry. Pacing your breath on purpose interrupts that loop at the one point in it you can actually reach with a decision.

The timer above is set to 4-7-8: in for four seconds, hold for seven, out for eight. That works out at a little over three breaths a minute, and the out-breath is twice as long as the in-breath. If the hold feels unpleasant — and for a lot of anxious people it does — use the buttons under the timer to switch to extended exhale or cyclic sighing. Neither of those holds the breath at all, and neither is a lesser version of the practice. Nothing on this page asks you to stay with the pattern it opened on.

Why the exhale is the long part

Your heart rate is not steady across a single breath. It rises slightly as you breathe in and falls again as you breathe out. That rise and fall is called respiratory sinus arrhythmia, and Zaccaro and colleagues' 2018 systematic review of slow breathing in Frontiers in Human Neuroscience describes it as an index of the parasympathetic side of the nervous system — the calming side — writing that "RSA on its side is consistently considered a robust index of parasympathetic activity". Making the out-breath the long part of the cycle means spending more of each breath in the half where the heart is slowing rather than speeding up. That is the usual explanation for why calming patterns tend to be built around a long exhale rather than a long inhale, and it is a plausible mechanism rather than a settled one: what the trials actually measure is how people say they feel, not what any one nerve did.

Slowing down matters on its own too, and this is the better-evidenced half of the story. The same review draws the line for the whole category at "slow breathing techniques (<10 breaths/minute)" — against a typical adult resting rate of roughly twelve to twenty — and reports that these techniques "promote autonomic changes increasing Heart Rate Variability and Respiratory Sinus Arrhythmia paralleled by Central Nervous System (CNS) activity modifications". It also notes that "HRV modulation is highly dependent on the respiration frequency, increasing along with the slowing of breath", which is the plain-language reason a pattern is built around a count rather than left to feel. A pattern like 4-7-8, at a little over three breaths a minute, or a plain five seconds in and five seconds out at six, puts you well inside that band without you having to measure your own rate or think about it. The counting does the work. That is really all a guided timer is for: it holds the count so your attention does not have to, which is a genuine relief when your attention is the thing that is misbehaving. The research page sets out what this literature does and does not establish.

How to do it

1. Sit somewhere you can stay for five minutes

Upright, feet on the floor, back supported if there is something to lean on. Lying down is fine too. Do not do this while driving, cycling, on a ladder or in water. If you are in a meeting or on a bus, you can do the whole thing without moving or making a sound.

2. Let the breath drop lower

Put one hand on your belly for a few breaths and let that hand be the one that moves. Anxious breathing tends to sit high in the chest, so this is often the biggest single change, and it is worth ten seconds before you start counting anything.

3. Breathe in through your nose for four

Follow the circle as it expands. Steady and unhurried, not a big dramatic gulp. If four seconds already feels like too much air, take less — the count matters more than the volume.

4. Hold, gently, for seven

Gently is the operative word. This is a pause with air in you, not a strain or a clamp. If seven seconds is uncomfortable, shorten it to three or four, or skip the hold entirely by switching to extended exhale. Nobody is marking this.

5. Breathe out through your mouth for eight

Lips slightly pursed, as though you were cooling a spoonful of soup, so the air leaves slowly and evenly. Let the exhale be soft rather than forced. Getting to the end of eight without running out of air is the part that improves with practice.

6. Repeat, and expect the first minute to feel like nothing

Five minutes is about fifteen breaths at this pace. The first few are usually taken up with getting the counting right. Give it to the end of the timer before you decide whether it did anything.

When to use it, and when not to

Breathing is a small tool, and as anxiety relief goes it has one practical advantage: it is available wherever you already are, with nothing to arrange and nobody to ask.

It fits well in the twenty minutes before something you are dreading — a call, an interview, a scan, a conversation you have been putting off. It fits at three in the morning when you are awake and your mind has started making lists. It fits in a waiting room, in a parked car before you go in, or after an argument when your body is still running on adrenaline and the argument itself is over. It also works best when it is not reserved for emergencies: a five-minute session at a fixed point in the day is how the pattern becomes something your body already knows, rather than a new skill you are trying to learn at the worst possible moment.

There are times to reach for something else. In the middle of a full panic attack, a seven-second hold is usually the wrong instruction, and a page written for that specific situation will serve you better — read breathing exercises for panic attacks instead. If you notice you are using breathing exercises to avoid a situation entirely, rather than to get through it, that is worth mentioning to a therapist, because avoidance is the mechanism that keeps anxiety in place. And if breathing has quietly turned into a ritual you must complete a set number of times or something bad will happen, that is a different problem and a timer will not fix it.

Who should be careful with the hold

4-7-8 contains a seven-second breath-hold, and breath-holds are not for everyone. This is not a scary-warning formality — please read it.

  • Speak to a clinician before practicing breath-holds if you are pregnant, or if you live with a cardiovascular condition, uncontrolled high blood pressure, epilepsy, a respiratory condition such as asthma or COPD, glaucoma, or you have had surgery recently.
  • If you live with panic attacks, the air hunger from a hold can feel like the beginning of one. Use extended exhale or cyclic sighing instead, both of which are on the buttons above and hold nothing.
  • Practice sitting or lying down. Never while driving, cycling, on a ladder or in water.
  • Stop if you feel dizzy, light-headed, tingly or short of breath, and go back to breathing normally. Feeling worse is a reason to stop, not a reason to push through.
  • If a pattern leaves you gasping at the end of the exhale, the pattern is too long for you today. Shorten it. There is nothing to be gained from finishing the count.

What the evidence shows

Honestly: modest, real, and not a cure. The 2018 review quoted further up reported that across the studies it covered, slow breathing was associated with "increased comfort, relaxation, pleasantness, vigor and alertness, and reduced symptoms of arousal, anxiety, depression, anger, and confusion" — a list of self-reported states, gathered from small studies using several different protocols. Balban and colleagues' 2023 randomized controlled trial in Cell Reports Medicine put 108 people through 28 days of five-minute daily practice — three different breathwork patterns against an equal amount of mindfulness meditation — and reported that "Cyclic sighing is most effective at improving mood and reducing respiratory rate". That was the pattern in the trial with the longest, most deliberate exhale. And Fincham and colleagues' 2023 meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials in Scientific Reports found, across twenty trials measuring anxiety, "a significant small-medium between-group ES in favour of breathwork, g = − 0.32", while noting that "Most studies were deemed as being at moderate risk of bias" and that its authors "urge caution and advocate for nuanced research approaches with low risk-of-bias study designs to avoid a miscalibration between hype and evidence". So: a real but small-to-medium effect on how anxious people say they feel, drawn from a literature that is not yet as clean as anyone would like. That is a good reason to give five minutes of it a try, and not a reason to change anything else you are doing about your anxiety.

Two things that paragraph deliberately does not say. It does not say breathing treats an anxiety disorder — none of those papers tested that, and this timer makes no such claim. And it does not say one pattern is right for you. The trial that favored cyclic sighing measured group averages over a month; you are one person on one afternoon, and the pattern you can actually stay with for five minutes is the one that will do you the most good.

Sources: Zaccaro et al., Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 2018 · Balban et al., Cell Reports Medicine, 2023 · Fincham et al., Scientific Reports, 2023

Common questions

Will a breathing exercise make my anxiety go away?

No, and it is worth saying plainly. Paced breathing is a way of turning the volume down on the physical side of anxiety for a few minutes. It does not treat an anxiety disorder, it does not resolve whatever you are anxious about, and it is not a substitute for therapy or medication if those are what you need. What it can do is give you a few minutes of steadier body while you decide what to do next.

How long before I feel anything?

Most people notice something in the second or third minute rather than the first. The first minute is usually taken up with getting the counting right and noticing how uncomfortable it is to slow down. That is why the timer opens at five minutes. If you stop at ninety seconds because nothing has happened yet, you stopped just before the part that helps.

The seven-second hold makes me feel worse. Am I doing it wrong?

You are not doing it wrong. Holding the breath produces air hunger, and if you are already anxious your body can read that as the start of something bad. Shorten the hold to two or three seconds, or drop it entirely and switch to extended exhale or cyclic sighing with the buttons on this page. Neither pattern holds the breath at all, and neither is a lesser option.

Should I breathe through my nose or my mouth?

The usual instruction is in through the nose and out through the mouth, with the lips slightly pursed so the exhale stays slow. It is a preference, not a rule. If your nose is blocked, or if mouth breathing feels calmer to you, breathe whichever way lets you keep the count without straining. Keeping the rhythm matters more than the route.

How often should I practice?

Five minutes once a day is a reasonable target, at a fixed time rather than only when you feel bad. Practicing when you are calm is what makes the pattern available when you are not, because you are not trying to learn a new skill during the worst moment of your week. If a daily habit feels like too much, three times a week is still worth doing.

Can I use this instead of seeing someone about my anxiety?

No. If anxiety is affecting your sleep, your work, your relationships or your body most days, that is worth talking to a doctor or a therapist about, and a free timer on a website is not a stand-in for that conversation. Use this alongside proper care, not in place of it. If you are in crisis, use one of the helplines at the top of this page.
