Two minutes, then the work

The timer above runs box breathing for two minutes: in for four, hold for four, out for four, hold for four. It is built for the moment right before something demanding — the document you have been circling all morning, the code review, the call you would rather not make — and then it gets out of your way.

Focus is not a switch you can flip. What you can do is turn down the things competing with it. Two minutes of paced breathing gives your attention one simple external thing to follow, and it draws a line between whatever you were doing before and the thing you are about to do. Most people looking for breathing exercises for focus are not after a meditation practice. They are after a way to stop scrolling and start.

Box breathing suits a desk because it is boring in the best way. Four equal counts, no special posture, no audible sighing, nothing anyone on a video call would notice. It is a breathing exercise you can do at your desk with your hands still resting on the keyboard, which is more than can be said for most things filed under mindfulness.

How to do it

Leave the length at two minutes, press Begin, and follow the circle. If you would rather not look at a screen, open the settings and turn the sound on, then close your eyes and follow the tones instead.

  1. Sit back. Feet flat, forearms resting, shoulders dropped. You do not need a straight-backed meditation posture. You need to stop bracing.
  2. Breathe out first. Empty your lungs gently before the first count, so the pattern starts from the bottom instead of from a half-held breath.
  3. In through your nose for four. Let the air go low, so your belly moves before your chest does. Nose breathing keeps it quiet and slows you down on its own.
  4. Hold for four. Throat open, jaw soft, shoulders down. This is a pause, not a clench. If you notice yourself gripping, shorten the hold.
  5. Out for four. Slow and even, through the nose or slightly pursed lips, as if you were fogging a window in slow motion.
  6. Hold empty for four, then start the next round. Two minutes is about seven or eight rounds, which is plenty.
  7. Begin the task within a minute of the timer ending. This step matters more than the pattern does. The point is to hand yourself over to the work, not to sit there feeling pleasantly settled while the tab count climbs again.

If four counts feel long, use three — that is triangle breathing, and it has its own timer. If four feel short, five works too. The exact count matters far less than keeping the exhale unhurried and the whole thing comfortable. The longer walkthrough of the pattern, including where it came from and how to lengthen it, is on the box breathing page.

When to use it, and when not to

Good moments to use breathing for concentration:

  • Right before a block of deep work, as a deliberate starting line.
  • Between two meetings, when the last one is still running in your head.
  • After an interruption, when getting back in costs more than the interruption did.
  • Before a difficult conversation you have already rehearsed enough.
  • Mid-afternoon, instead of a third coffee you did not really want.

Poor moments, in plain terms:

  • When you are already drowsy. A slow, settling pattern is not built to wake you up, and it can leave you feeling more sluggish than you started. Daylight, water and a short walk first.
  • When the real problem is that the task is undefined. No breathing pattern will tell you what the next action is. Write the next action down, then breathe.
  • When what you feel is closer to panic than to distraction. Breath-holds are the wrong tool there. Use the hold-free approach on breathing for panic attacks instead.
  • When you are hungry, dehydrated or on four hours of sleep. Breathing is not a substitute for the boring inputs, and on those days it will not feel like it does anything.

Before you use the holds

Box breathing contains two four-second breath-holds, and the holds are the part worth taking seriously. Skip them, or talk to your doctor before using this pattern, if any of these apply to you:

  • Pregnancy.
  • A cardiovascular condition, or high blood pressure that is not well controlled.
  • Epilepsy or any seizure disorder.
  • A respiratory condition such as asthma or COPD, especially during a flare-up.
  • Recent surgery, particularly abdominal, chest or eye surgery.
  • Glaucoma or raised pressure in the eye.
  • Panic disorder or a history of panic attacks — breath-holding is a common trigger.

Dropping the holds leaves you with a slow, even in-and-out, which is a perfectly good focus pattern by itself: five in and five out is heart coherence breathing. Stop and breathe normally if you feel dizzy or light-headed, if your hands or lips tingle, or if the pattern makes you want to gasp. None of this should be uncomfortable, and there is nothing to gain from pushing through discomfort here.

What the evidence shows

The honest picture is smaller than the internet suggests, and the four studies closest to what this page asks you to do are worth reading carefully rather than quoting loosely.

The nearest test of this exact pattern is a randomized trial published in Comprehensive Psychoneuroendocrinology in 2026. Sixty-six people were assigned to normal breathing, a prolonged exhale, or box breathing at the same four-second ratio the timer above uses, then put through a virtual version of a standard laboratory stress test. The stress test raised heart rate, state anxiety and salivary alpha-amylase in everyone. In both breathing groups those rises were smaller than in the group that breathed normally — the authors write that both conditions "attenuated post-stressor increases in HR, SAI, and sAA compared to NB". The breathing blunted the stress response rather than preventing it. Cognitive performance was statistically unchanged across all three groups. The breathing settled the body somewhat without shifting what people could actually do.

A laboratory study by Röttger and colleagues in Applied Psychophysiology and Biofeedback (2021) makes the same point more sharply. Thirty healthy people worked through a Stroop interference task under time pressure and noise distraction. Each of them used both patterns, in a counterbalanced order: tactical breathing — the paced technique taught to military and law-enforcement personnel — and a simple prolonged exhale. How stressed people said they felt did not differ between the two. Physiological arousal was lower with tactical breathing, but performance on the task was better with the prolonged exhale. A calmer body is not automatically a sharper one, so if raw performance under pressure is what you are after, a pattern that simply makes the out-breath longer than the in-breath may be worth trying instead of a square. The closest one on this site is the cyclic sighing timer: two seconds in, a short second sip on top, then six seconds out, with nothing held anywhere.

Regular practice looks more promising than a one-off. In a trial of forty healthy adults published in Frontiers in Psychology (2017), half were trained in diaphragmatic breathing across twenty sessions over eight weeks, at an average of four breaths a minute. That group's sustained attention rose significantly compared with its own baseline, and its cortisol fell. Worth saying plainly: the control group's attention scores also rose, which is what tends to happen when people sit the same test repeatedly. The clearer separation between the two groups was in cortisol, which dropped in the trained group and did not move in the control group. Either way, that is a training effect measured over two months, at a much slower breathing rate than the pattern here, and it should not be read as a promise about the next two minutes.

Zooming out, a systematic review in Frontiers in Human Neuroscience (2018) narrowed a pool of 2,461 abstracts down to fifteen studies of slow breathing — under ten breaths a minute — in healthy people. Its summary of the psychological picture is increased comfort, relaxation, pleasantness, vigor and alertness, alongside reduced symptoms of arousal, anxiety, depression, anger and confusion. That is a modest and fairly consistent set of effects, measured through questionnaires and physiology rather than through anyone's actual work.

So: slow, paced breathing reliably changes how your body feels, and for many people that change is what makes starting easier. It is not a cognitive enhancer, nothing here treats or manages a medical condition, and any page promising a focus boost from two minutes of anything is running ahead of the evidence.

Common questions

How long should I breathe before starting work?

Two minutes is enough for most people, which is why this page starts there. Longer is not obviously better for focus. Past about five minutes you are practicing, which is worth doing, but it is a different activity from clearing the runway for one task.

Is box breathing meant to make me alert or relaxed?

Mostly it lowers physical arousal, and the useful side effect is that your attention has less to compete with. If what you actually need is to feel more awake, daylight, water and a two-minute walk are more likely to help than any breathing pattern.

Can I do this at my desk without it being obvious?

Yes. Keep your eyes open and soft, breathe through your nose, and let the circle do the counting. From the outside it looks like you are reading something slowly. In an open-plan office nobody notices.

What if holding my breath makes me anxious?

Then drop the holds. Breathe in for four and out for six, or use the three-count triangle pattern, which holds for a much shorter time. A pattern that makes you tense is not doing its job, and there is nothing to push through here.

How often should I use a focus breathing exercise?

Once or twice a day, attached to a moment that already exists in your day: the start of your first deep-work block, or the gap after lunch. Anchoring it to something you already do is what makes it stick. A reminder at a random hour rarely survives a week.

Does breathing actually improve concentration?

Not reliably from a single short session. In the trial closest to this page's pattern, box breathing calmed heart rate and anxiety but left cognitive performance unchanged. The evidence is a little better for regular practice over weeks. Slow breathing does settle the body, and whether that turns into better work depends on what you do in the five minutes afterwards.
