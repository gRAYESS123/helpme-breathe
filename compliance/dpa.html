0. Read this part first

Most data processing addendums exist because a supplier holds a great deal of personal data on a customer's behalf. This one exists for the opposite reason: to write down, in a form you can file, that Help Me Breathe does not receive your clients' personal data at all.

The Help Me Breathe timer, the embedded widget and the client session links all run inside the browser of whoever opened them. There is no client record. Nothing about your clients, classes or students is stored by the Provider, nothing reaches a server about what anybody practised, and your clients are never asked to create an account. So for the data you are most likely to be worried about, which is who your clients are, what they did, when and how often, there is no processing to describe, because none of it reaches us.

What does exist, and what this version of the addendum adds, is your own account. When you subscribe there is an account, a subscription record and a free-trial entry, held in a database. Clause 5 lists every field, clause 7 names the database provider and clause 14 gives the retention numbers. All of it is about you, the practitioner. None of it is about anybody you work with.

Help Me Breathe is also not a company. It is one person, Georges Rayess, resident in Lebanon, trading under that name. There is no legal entity, no data protection officer, no compliance department and no audited security programme. This document is written to be accurate about that rather than to look like something larger. If your organisation needs a supplier with SOC 2, a HIPAA Business Associate Agreement, ISO 27001 or a completed vendor questionnaire, this addendum will not satisfy it and you should not subscribe.

3. Roles

You are the controller of any personal data relating to your clients.

For the account and subscription data in clause 5, the Provider acts as a controller in its own right, because it decides what that data is for: running your subscription, keeping the free trial to one per person, and administering its own business records and invoices. The privacy policy sets out the legal basis for each of those purposes, one basis per purpose. Version 1.0 of this addendum called the Provider a processor of that same data, which sat badly beside the privacy policy, and this version says the true thing instead. The obligations in clauses 9 and 10 are given to you contractually either way, and none of them is weakened by the correction.

In respect of your clients' personal data, the Provider is neither a controller nor a processor, because it does not receive that data. Clause 6 sets out how that works technically, and the privacy attestation states it as a signed, dated statement.

5. The only personal data that flows anywhere

There are six flows, and every one of them concerns you rather than your clients. Checkout is not open yet, so five of the six describe what happens when you subscribe.

  • Your account and how you sign in. An account is opened against your email address. You sign in with a one-time email link, a one-time six-digit code, or a Google account. There is no password, so there is no password for the Provider to store or lose. The account records your email address, your sign-in identities and the date it was created. Your browser keeps a short-lived signed token so that an outage does not interrupt a session. Neither the account nor the token records what you practised.
  • Your email address at the merchant of record. When you subscribe, the merchant of record takes the payment as the seller on record and holds your email address, your payment details and the country and tax information it needs to charge the right amount. It is a controller of that payment record in its own right, not the Provider's processor for it. The Provider never sees your card number, its expiry date or its security code.
  • Your subscription record. The Provider stores the plan, its status, its period dates, the payment provider's subscription and customer identifiers, and the amount and currency that provider actually charges you, taken from the provider rather than assumed. The identifiers are what make it possible to match a receipt, a refund or a dispute to the right account. The Provider also keeps the messages the payment provider sends about your subscription, so that a missed or disputed one can be checked against what happened.
  • The free-trial check. The rule is one free trial per person. To keep it, the Provider stores a one-way hash of your email address, computed on its own server with a secret key and not reversible into an address, and a random identifier held in a first-party cookie on your device, __Host-hmb_did, which lasts 2 years. That hash is still personal data rather than anonymous data, because the Provider holds the key and could confirm a match against an address it already had, and it is treated as personal data throughout this addendum. There is no browser fingerprinting of any kind, no reading of your screen, fonts, time zone or hardware, and no device profile. The check can only ever withdraw the trial offer. It never denies you access and it never affects your clients, who are not checked at all.
  • Embed credentials, if you mint one. The Provider stores the credential's identifiers, the domains you registered, the dates it was issued and expires, and a count of how many times it was verified. The count is against your credential. Nothing about the visitor who loaded the widget is recorded, on your site or anywhere else.
  • Email you send. If you write to contact@helpmebreath.com, the Provider has your message and your address, in an ordinary email mailbox, for as long as that correspondence is useful.

The account, subscription, trial and credential records above sit in one database, named in clause 7. The browser never reads or writes it directly: every access goes through the Provider's own server, using a secret key that stays on the server, and the tables grant a browser nothing. Your email to the Provider sits in an ordinary mailbox rather than in that database.

6. What is not processed, and why

None of your clients' personal data is transmitted to, stored by, or accessible to the Provider. In detail:

  • There is no client account, no client profile and no client identifier. Nothing on the site asks for a client's name, email address, date of birth or health information.
  • The timer runs entirely in the browser. Session settings, session history and streaks are written to that browser's own local storage under the hmb. prefix and are never transmitted anywhere. On helpmebreath.com itself, where the first few sessions on a device are free before an account is needed, the page tells the server that a session finished so that the allowance can be counted. That message is a count against the device cookie. It carries nothing about the session and nothing that identifies a person, and neither the embedded widget nor a client session link sends it, because neither has an allowance.
  • A co-branded client session link carries its whole configuration inside the link itself — the technique, the length, your name, your message. Opening it writes nothing to any server, and no record exists of which link was created, sent, or opened. It does load the site's typefaces from Google Fonts, so Google's font servers see the reader's IP address and user agent in the ordinary way, and a practitioner logo, where the link carries one, is fetched by the reader's browser from the practitioner's own server. Neither request carries anything about the session. The embedded widget under /embed/v1/ makes no third-party request at all, so use it instead where that matters.
  • The embedded widget makes no third-party requests of any kind: no fonts, no analytics, no advertising and no tracking pixels load inside the frame.
  • Class and projector mode, and the printable handouts, are entirely local. Practitioner details typed into the handout page are stored in that browser under hmb.practitioner and are never sent to the Provider.

There is one unavoidable exception, and it is stated plainly rather than buried: like every website, the pages are served over the internet, so the hosting provider's servers necessarily see the requesting IP address, the user agent string and the URL requested in order to return the page. That is ordinary web serving, it is described in clause 7, and together with the font and logo requests noted above, and the free-session count on the main site, it is the whole of what leaves a client's browser.

8. International transfers

The Provider is resident in Lebanon. Lebanon does not benefit from a European Commission adequacy decision. Three things follow, and all three are stated openly.

First, no client personal data is transferred anywhere, because none of it is collected. For the data that matters most in a therapeutic or educational setting, the transfer question does not arise.

Second, the account data in clause 5 is stored in the European Union, in the database region named in clause 7, and is not moved out of it by the Provider. Hosting and the merchant of record are in the United States and operate under their own published transfer terms.

Third, that account data does reach Lebanon, because that is where the Provider signs in to administer it and reads email. Where you are established in the EEA, the UK or Switzerland and require a transfer mechanism for that, the Provider will sign the European Commission's Standard Contractual Clauses (Module Two, controller to processor) and the UK International Data Transfer Addendum on request, before you buy. Please ask first rather than after; the Provider will not claim to have safeguards already in place that have not been executed.

10. Security measures

The measures below are the ones actually in place. The security overview describes them at more length and is treated as an annex to this addendum.

  • The site is static, and the only personal data stored is the record described in clause 5. There is no store of client data to breach.
  • All traffic is served over TLS, with HTTP requests redirected to HTTPS by the host.
  • The application programming interface runs as serverless functions at the host. Every database access goes through them, using a secret key held only on the server, and the browser never talks to the database. Row level security is enabled and forced on every table, with no policy granting a browser anything, so a key that leaked into a page would still read nothing. The user's identity is always taken from the verified sign-in token, never from the body of a request.
  • Secrets live only in the hosting platform's encrypted environment variables: the token signing key, the database secret key, the two peppers behind the trial hash and the device identifier, and the provider API keys. They are not in the source repository and are not written to logs.
  • There are no passwords to leak, because there are none. Sign-in is a one-time email link, a one-time code, or a Google account. An email address is never written to logs, analytics or error reports in full.
  • Messages from the merchant of record are verified against its signature, with a constant-time comparison and a narrow timestamp window so an old message cannot be replayed, and each is applied exactly once.
  • Subscription tokens and embed credentials are signed with HMAC-SHA256 and carry a short expiry, so a tampered or stale one simply fails and the browser falls back to the free attributed timer. An embed credential can be revoked, which invalidates every copy of it at once.
  • Serverless functions apply best-effort per-address rate limiting.
  • Security headers are set at the edge: content type sniffing off, a restrictive referrer policy, a permissions policy denying camera, microphone and geolocation, and framing denied everywhere except the widget and client-link paths that are meant to be embedded.
  • The Provider's own accounts with the hosting platform, the database provider, the merchant of record and the email provider use unique passwords stored in a password manager and two-factor authentication.

11. Personal data breach

If the Provider becomes aware of a personal data breach affecting your data, it will notify you by email, to the address on your account, without undue delay and in any case within 72 hours of becoming aware of it. The notification will describe what happened, what data was involved, what the likely consequences are, and what is being done about it — including the parts that are not yet known, said as such rather than guessed at.

Because the Provider holds no client data, a breach of the Provider's systems cannot expose your clients' details. What such a breach could expose is the record in clause 5: your email address, your plan and its dates, the provider's identifiers, the one-way trial hash and your embed credentials. It could not expose a password, because there are none, and it could not expose a card number, because none is ever held here. A breach at the merchant of record could expose the billing information you gave them; they notify the Provider under their own terms, and the Provider will pass that notification on to you.

If a data subject contacts the Provider directly, the Provider will not respond substantively to a request that concerns your data. It will tell them to contact you and let you know it happened.

14. Return, retention and deletion

Your account page carries an export control and a delete control, and the Provider does the same thing by hand if you ask by email. The export hands you a file holding your account record, every subscription record with the provider's identifiers, and your embed credentials. It contains no hashes, no secret keys and nobody else's data.

Deletion runs in this order. If a subscription is live it is cancelled at the merchant of record first and the cancellation is confirmed, because an account deleted with billing still running is worse than no deletion at all. The subscription record is then detached rather than destroyed: your account identifier is removed and the provider's identifiers, the plan and the dates remain, so a later refund or dispute can still be matched to something. Your account, your sign-in identities and your embed credentials are deleted. Your account identifier is removed from the free-trial records. Deletion is refused, with a plain message, while a payment dispute is open or a failed payment is outstanding.

The retention periods, with their reasons:

  • Account data: kept while the account exists. On deletion it goes at once, and a copy may persist in the database provider's backups for up to 30 days.
  • Messages from the payment provider: the body of a processed message is kept 30 days and then blanked; the record that it arrived is kept 180 days. A message that could not be processed keeps its body until it has been, so that a missed payment can be put right.
  • The free-trial hash and the device record: 24 months from their last activity, then deleted. The reason, said plainly: otherwise deleting an account would reset the free-trial limit and the rule would mean nothing. Deleting your account unlinks these records from you but does not shorten the 24 months.
  • Embed credentials: kept while the account exists; an expired or rotated one is deleted 30 days after it stops working.
  • Invoices and tax records: kept by the merchant of record under its own law and its own policy, which is its to set rather than the Provider's.
  • Email correspondence: kept while it is useful, except where it is evidence of an unresolved dispute.

The merchant of record holds its own copy of the payment record as a separate controller. The Provider cannot erase that copy, and a request to erase it goes to the seller named on your receipt.

Your clients' data needs no deletion step, because it was never collected. Data in a client's own browser is deleted by that person clearing their browser storage, and the Provider cannot reach it in order to delete it for them.
