What box breathing is

Box breathing is four equal counts, repeated: breathe in for four, hold for four, breathe out for four, hold empty for four. The box breathing timer above is already set to that rhythm for five minutes. Press Begin and follow the circle — you do not have to count anything yourself.

The same pattern goes by several names. If you searched for a square breathing exercise, this is it: the word square describes the shape. Four-square breathing and 4-4-4-4 breathing describe the counts. Tactical breathing is the name it carries in military and police training; a 2024 trial protocol in Frontiers in Psychiatry introduces the exercise as box breathing, "also known as 'tactical breathing'" (Vetter and colleagues). They are all the same four sides of the same box, and the fact that every side is the same length is the whole point of it: there is no number to remember, no ratio to get wrong, and nothing that feels clever. Two rounds in, your body has the rhythm and your attention is free to go somewhere else.

It is widely taught to people who need composure under pressure. In the research literature the pattern is described, under the name tactical breathing, as one "used by military and law enforcement personnel" to reduce stress and keep psychomotor and cognitive performance intact in dangerous situations (Röttger and colleagues, Applied Psychophysiology and Biofeedback, 2021; PubMed abstract). We will not go a step further than that sentence, because a step further than that sentence is where breathing advice usually starts inventing things. What it tells you is modest and real: this is a rhythm chosen for situations where a person still has to function, not one that only works in a quiet room with the door shut. That is a genuinely useful property in a breathing exercise, and it is most of why the square has lasted.

How to do it

Five steps, sixteen seconds a round. Read them once, then let the circle do the counting.

1. Sit down and settle

Sit upright with your feet flat on the floor and your shoulders low. Rest your hands wherever they are comfortable. Close your eyes, or let them settle on one fixed point. Do this sitting or lying down — never while driving, cycling or in water.

2. Breathe in for four

Breathe in through your nose for a slow count of four, letting your belly move before your chest. Aim for smooth rather than deep. A box breath is not a big breath. If four seconds feels like a stretch, use three and build up later.

3. Hold for four

Hold with your lungs comfortably full for four counts. Keep your jaw, throat and shoulders soft — this is a pause, not a clench. You should feel no pressure behind your eyes or in your ears. If you do, you took in too much air on the way up.

4. Breathe out for four

Let the air out through your nose or your mouth over four even counts. Do not squeeze the last of it out; leave a little in reserve. A steady, unhurried exhale is what makes the next round easy to keep, and it is the part most people rush.

5. Hold empty for four, then go again

Pause for four counts with your lungs empty, then begin the next round. One full square takes sixteen seconds, so a five-minute session is a little under nineteen rounds. You do not need to finish the session for it to have been worth starting.

When to use it, and when not to

Good moments for a square

Box breathing earns its keep in the waiting. The ten minutes before an interview, a scan, a presentation or a difficult call. The gap after an argument, when you are still lit up and there is nothing useful left to say. The reset between two blocks of work, where an even rhythm clears the last task out of your head — that is the use case behind our breathing exercises for focus page, which opens the same pattern at a shorter length.

It also holds up in places where a more elaborate pattern would not. Because the counts are equal, you can keep the square going while standing in a queue, sitting in a waiting room or riding a lift, without looking like you are doing anything at all.

When something else fits better

For falling asleep, the even square is a steadying rhythm rather than a winding-down one. A longer exhale suits the end of the day better — try 4-7-8 breathing or a plain extended exhale instead.

In the middle of a rising panic, deliberately holding your breath can feel like the wrong direction entirely, and for some people the empty hold is the exact sensation they are frightened of. Something continuous, with no pause in it, is usually kinder in that moment.

And if you are about to do something demanding rather than wait through it, the small amount of evidence we have suggests a long exhale may serve you better than a square. That is in the evidence section below, along with what the studies did not show.

Before you hold your breath

This pattern holds the breath twice in every round — once with the lungs full, once with them empty. For most people that is unremarkable. For some it is a real consideration, so please read this part properly.

  • Speak to a clinician before practising breath-holds if you are pregnant, or if you live with a cardiovascular condition, uncontrolled high blood pressure, epilepsy, a respiratory condition such as asthma or COPD, glaucoma, or you have had surgery recently.
  • If you live with panic attacks, the empty hold in particular can feel like the beginning of one. Shorten it to one or two counts, or use extended exhale instead, where nothing is ever held.
  • Practise sitting or lying down. Never while driving, cycling, swimming or in water.
  • Stop if you feel dizzy, light-headed, tingling in your hands or face, or short of breath. Breathe normally, let it pass completely, and if you try again, use shorter counts and a smaller inhale.
  • If you are being treated for anything at all, this is an addition to your care and never a substitute for it. Nothing on this page treats or cures a condition.

What the evidence shows

Honestly: less than the internet implies, and enough to be interesting. Box breathing was one of three breathwork arms in a remote, randomised, month-long trial of five minutes of daily practice, compared against an equal amount of mindfulness meditation and published in Cell Reports Medicine in 2023 (Balban and colleagues). Every arm did well on the everyday measures: the paper reports that "Both mindfulness meditation and breathwork groups showed significant reductions in state anxiety and negative affect and increases in positive affect". Where the groups separated, though, the arm that pulled clearly away from meditation on positive affect was cyclic sighing — not box breathing, for which that difference was not statistically significant. The authors are candid about the limits. People practised unsupervised at home, so how closely they followed the instructions could not be checked. Each group was small enough to limit the power to compare the breathwork arms against one another. And the whole thing ran four weeks, with no follow-up afterwards.

The other useful piece of work looked at box breathing under its tactical name. Thirty healthy participants did a Stroop interference task under time pressure while white noise climbed from 77 to 89 decibels, using either tactical breathing or a simple prolonged exhale (Röttger and colleagues, 2021; PubMed abstract). The result is the interesting part, because it cuts both ways: tactical breathing gave the participants "a lower physiological arousal", but the prolonged exhale gave them the better score on the task. The authors' reading is that the square may suit passive coping — waiting, enduring, holding yourself together — while a long exhale suits active coping, where you have something to do. A separate randomised study put 100 student soldiers in a shooting simulator and found the tactical breathing group averaged 1.9 points better on the harder first shot (Ibrahim and colleagues, 2024; PubMed abstract). That is one narrow task in a simulator, and it says nothing about how anyone feels a week later.

So: small samples, short studies, one specific task at a time, and no evidence that box breathing treats any condition. What it has going for it is that it is free, it takes five minutes, the risks are limited and clearly known, and people keep doing it. Adjusting the count

Four is a convention, not a law. If the holds feel long, drop the whole square to three: 3-3-3-3 is the same shape with less air debt, and it is what our triangle breathing timer does with the empty hold removed entirely. If four feels short and the rhythm has become automatic, five or six a side slows you to between two and a half and three breaths a minute, which is slow enough that you will notice it.

You can also break the square deliberately. Keeping the inhale at four and stretching the exhale to six, with short holds or none, gives you something calmer for the evening. What you should not do is lengthen the holds while keeping a big inhale — that is the combination that produces the light-headedness people mistake for the technique working.

Common questions

How long should I do box breathing for?

Three to five minutes is a sensible first session, which is why the timer above opens at five. That is long enough to settle into the rhythm and short enough that you will actually do it again tomorrow. If you are using it to steady yourself before something specific, one minute is still worth doing.

Is box breathing the same as square breathing or tactical breathing?

Yes. Box breathing, square breathing, four-square breathing and tactical breathing all describe the same equal-count pattern. Tactical breathing is the name used in military and police training, where the rhythm is taught for staying composed under pressure.

Do I have to breathe through my nose?

Nasal breathing on the way in is the usual instruction and it slows the inhale for you. If your nose is blocked, breathe through your mouth and keep the counts even. The rhythm matters more than the route.

Why do I feel light-headed during the holds?

Usually because the inhale is too big or the counts are too long for you right now. Drop to a three-count square, make the inhale smaller and softer, and let the holds be genuinely relaxed. If light-headedness continues, stop, breathe normally, and read the cautions on this page before trying again.

Can I use box breathing to fall asleep?

You can, but the even square is a steadying rhythm rather than a winding-down one. For sleep, most people get on better with a pattern where the exhale is clearly longer than the inhale, such as 4-7-8 or a simple extended exhale.

What is the difference between box breathing and 4-7-8?

Box breathing uses four equal counts and holds the breath twice per round, which makes it easy to remember and useful when you need to stay level. 4-7-8 has a long exhale and one hold, which suits winding down at night.
